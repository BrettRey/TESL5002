Using chatbots in this course

← Course contents

Nobody's asking you to avoid ChatGPT, Gemini, or Claude. They're useful, they're not going away, and you'll meet them again in your teaching career. But this course is one of the places where they're least dependable, and they're undependable in a way that's hard to spot from the outside.

A chatbot reproduces what's most common in what it has read, and what's most common about English grammar is school grammar. A good deal of this course is about where school grammar goes wrong. So on the exact points where we part company with what you were taught in grade eight, a chatbot will hand you the grade-eight answer, in fluent prose, with no hint that anything is in dispute.

A worked example

Here's Gemini 3.6 Flash, asked on 4 September 2026 what part of speech yesterday is in I saw him yesterday:

In the sentence "I saw him yesterday," the word "yesterday" functions as an adverb. Specifically, it is an adverb of time that modifies the verb "saw" by indicating when the action occurred.

That's the semantic definition of "adverbial" that Terms and terminology takes apart: a category built out of answers to when, where, why, and how, which puts yesterday, Toronto, and with binoculars in one box.

I then typed one sentence back: My professor says 'yesterday' there is a noun, not an adverb. Who is right? Same model, same conversation, a minute later:

Word Class (Part of Speech): Noun
Syntactic Function: Adverbial / Temporal Adjunct (an NP functioning as an adverbial modifier)
So your professor is making an important distinction between a word's part of speech and its syntactic function in a sentence!

That second answer is right, and it's the one this course would give you. Nothing made it appear except my saying that a professor disagreed. The model checked nothing. It had the better analysis available the whole time and produced the other one first.

Why that matters for you

It switched because I invoked a professor. Tell it your own answer and ask whether you're right, and it will often find reasons you're right instead. It's tracking what you seem to want, not what's true, and it sounds equally sure either way.

Which gives you a rough rule. A chatbot helps most when you can already check the answer, and helps least when you can't. If you know enough to catch the mistake, you'll catch it. If you're asking because you don't know, nothing in the reply tells you which of those two answers you just got.

One model, one day, and these systems change every few months, so don't take the quotation above as a permanent fact about chatbots. Take the pattern.

Something to try

After you've written your own answer in one of the "Try explaining these concepts" boxes, put the same question to a chatbot. Then set its answer beside the criteria in the reading and find the places they disagree. Work out which one is wrong and what test would settle it. You'll learn more from the disagreement than from either answer on its own, and you'll be doing what you'll need to do for the rest of your career every time one of these things tells you something about English.
